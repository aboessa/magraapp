## 🔴 The Water Safety Item

Drowning in shallow water is possible for young children, and it is **silent and fast**.

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| Depth | 🔴 **No deeper than a hand's palm** |
| Container | Wide and low · 🔴 **no bucket, no sink, no pail** |
| Presence | 🔴 **No child is left alone near the water, not even for a second** |
| Little ones | 🔴 The experiment is **postponed** if a child younger than 3 is in the room |
| Slipping | Keep a towel ready · wipe at once |
| Ending | The parent empties the basin **immediately** after the experiment |

## The Scientific Concept

**Floating is not decided by size.** It is decided by how heavy an object is **relative to its size** compared to water.

**Targeted misconception:** "The big one sinks and the small one floats." — the large apple floats, and the small key sinks.

**What we say to the child:** "Not the size, but its heaviness with its size." — the term "density" is deferred to the Junior stage.

## Parent Guide

> **🔴 Before you begin — the water item**
> - **The water is no deeper than a hand's palm.** A wide, low basin, no bucket, pail, or sink.
> - **Do not leave a child alone near a container of water, not even for a second, even if it is shallow.** Drowning in the young is silent and fast.
> - If there is a child younger than three in the room, **postpone the experiment**.
> - **Empty the basin immediately** after the experiment ends, not a little later.
> - Keep a towel ready. A wet floor is slippery.
>
> **What exactly do you do?**
> You pour the water and control its depth, and empty it at the end. The child lowers the objects **gently**, observes, and dries.
>
> **What is your child learning?**
> That **size is not the reason for floating**. The big apple floats, and the small key sinks. The reason is the object's heaviness **relative to its size**.
>
> **Why the apple and the key specifically?**
> Because "the big sinks and the small floats" is one of the most famous misconceptions at this age. We chose two objects that break it in a single shot, and showed them **together in one frame** so the evidence is in front of their eyes, not in their memory.
>
> **The wording is hard, and that is normal**
> "Light for its size" is a compound idea. Don't expect your child to phrase it precisely; it is enough that they **reject** the size rule. The scientific name (density) comes in the Junior stage.
>
> **What if their prediction is wrong?**
> That is what we want. Tell them: "Your prediction is part of science."

## Family Activity

> **The Floating Lab — 20 minutes**
>
> 1. **The grown-up pours water a palm deep.** Keep a towel ready.
> 2. Gather 10 large, safe objects from home.
> 3. The child **predicts first** for each object, then lowers it gently.
> 4. Record on a sheet: prediction · result · did they match?
> 5. Line up the floating objects from lightest to heaviest, and look: is the order tied to size?
>
> 🔴 **Constant presence. A palm deep. Objects bigger than the child's fist. Immediate emptying at the end.**
>
> **A lovely extra experiment:** put in an orange with its peel — it **floats**. Then peel it and put it back — it **sinks**.
>
> The same orange, now smaller in size, but it sank. The peel has air pockets that make the orange **light for its size**. This is the strongest experiment for breaking the size rule, and can be done after the episode.
>
> **A clever question:** "A ship is made of iron. Why does it float?" — because it is hollow, with a lot of air, so it became light for its size.
