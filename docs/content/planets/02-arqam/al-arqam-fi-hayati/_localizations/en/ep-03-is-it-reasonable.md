## The Question It Answers

> **"I calculated and got a strange number… how do I know it is wrong before I check?"**

## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child judges the reasonableness of the answer **before** the exact calculation, by rounding the numbers and setting a range the answer must fall within.

Criterion: sets a correct reasonable range in 4 of 5 problems, and rejects an impossible answer stating the reason in at least 3.

## New Vocabulary

| Word | How it is introduced |
|---|---|
| estimation | a close answer we get quickly and without paper |
| rounding | turning a number into an easier number close to it |
| the reasonable range | between the smallest possible answer and the largest possible answer |
| an impossible answer | a number that cannot be correct no matter how you calculate |

## Mastery Criterion

```
Sets a correct reasonable range in 4 of 5 problems before calculating,
rejects an impossible answer stating the reason in at least 3,
and states the rounding direction (up or down) at least once.
Giving an exact number instead of a range does not count as estimation.
```

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> **To judge their answer before trusting it.** Estimation is not an extra exercise; it is the safety barrier: a child who estimates first will not write an answer ten times too big and fail to notice.
>
> **The red line**
> Do not accept "I guessed" as a justification. Estimation has a reason: I rounded to this and it became that. A guess with no reason is not a mathematical skill.
>
> **How to help?**
> Before any calculation at home, ask: "Before you calculate, roughly how much?" — a simple habit built over weeks. And do not correct their estimate if it is close; an estimate is not required to be exact.
>
> **When to review with them?**
> If they give an exact number whenever asked to estimate, they have not grasped the difference. Replay scene three on its own and focus on "a region, not a number".

---

## Family Activity

> **Estimate Then Check — 15 minutes**
>
> 1. Choose **three things that can actually be counted** in the home: hallway tiles, books on a shelf, spoons in a drawer.
> 2. **Before counting**, your child writes an estimate with **its reason**: "A row has 10 and we have 6 rows, so about 60."
> 3. Count for real, and write the exact number.
> 4. Compute the difference. **A small difference is a success for the estimate**, not a failure.
> 5. **The counter-game:** you write an impossible answer to a problem, and your child spots it and explains why it is impossible.
>
> **No timer.** Estimation is trained on reasons, not on speed.
>
> **Variation for an older child:** after estimating, set **two bounds**: the smallest possible number and the largest possible number, then check that the exact one is between them.
