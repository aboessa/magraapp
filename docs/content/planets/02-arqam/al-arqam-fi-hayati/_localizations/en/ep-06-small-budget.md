## The Question It Answers

> **"I have a limited amount and I want everything. How do I decide?"**

## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child distributes a fixed amount across choices whose total cost exceeds it, **and explains what they gave up and why**.

Criterion: produces a distribution whose total does not exceed the amount in 4 of 5 attempts, and each time names **one choice they gave up and the reason for giving it up**.

## New Vocabulary

| Word | How it is introduced |
|---|---|
| the budget | a limited amount we distribute, and cannot exceed |
| the trade-off | to take one thing and leave another |
| the priority | what you choose first if the amount is not enough for everything |
| the remainder | what is left after distributing, which may be zero |

## Mastery Criterion

```
Produces a distribution whose total does not exceed the amount in 4 of 5 attempts,
each time names one choice they gave up and one reason for giving it up,
and states their priority before calculating at least once.
An arithmetically-correct distribution without explaining the trade-off does not count as mastery.
```

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> **That calculation serves a decision.** A budget is not addition and subtraction, but a trade-off: every "yes" means a "no" to something else. And your child learns to explain what they left out, a skill that grows with them.
>
> **The red line**
> Do not judge their choice as "bad" if they stayed within the budget. The episode does not prefer the book over the toy; it asks them to **state a priority and follow it**. And when you turn the activity into a moral lesson about thrift, the child loses the mathematics and the lesson together.
>
> **Why "unit" and not a currency?**
> On purpose. Real prices change and differ between countries, and what matters here is **the distribution skill**, not a number to memorize. Mention your own currency in the home activity if you wish.
>
> **How to help?**
> Give them a small real amount and a real decision that is theirs, and ask one thing: that before buying they say **what they will leave out and why**.

---

## Family Activity

> **The Unit Bazaar — 20 minutes**
>
> 1. Make **twenty slips of paper**, each one a "unit". This is the game's budget.
> 2. Write five cards for things around the home, and give each a cost in units — **so that the total is more than twenty**.
> 3. **Before any choice**, your child adds all the costs and writes: how much are they short?
> 4. They write their priority in one line, **then** choose their basket.
> 5. They explain: **what did you leave out, and why?** — this is the real task, not the basket.
> 6. **Swap roles:** you choose a basket that exceeds the budget by one unit, and let them spot it and compute the overrun.
>
> **The game's rule:** any distribution whose total is ≤ 20 is **acceptable**. There is no single "correct" basket, and the judgment is on the justification, not the choice.
>
> **Variation for an older child:** add one condition — "you must keep three units left" — and watch how the whole decision changes.
