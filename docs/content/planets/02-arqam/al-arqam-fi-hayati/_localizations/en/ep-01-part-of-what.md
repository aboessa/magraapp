## The Question It Answers

> **"Three quarters… three quarters of what?"**

## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child reads a fraction as a part of a **named whole**, stating what the whole is and into how many parts it was divided before stating the fraction's value.

Criterion: names the whole and identifies the number of equal parts in 5 of 6 displayed fractions, and detects the different whole in at least one comparison.

## New Vocabulary

| Word | How it is introduced |
|---|---|
| the whole | the complete thing we divide, which must be named |
| equal parts | pieces of the same size — otherwise they are not fractions |
| the denominator | into how many parts the whole was divided |
| the numerator | how many parts we are talking about |

## Mastery Criterion

```
Names the whole and identifies the number of equal parts in 5 of 6 displayed fractions,
refuses to call a part of unequal parts a "quarter",
and recognizes two differently-shaped, equal-valued fractions at least once.
Reading the fraction correctly without naming the whole does not count as mastery.
```

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> **That a fraction is a ratio, not a quantity.** "A half" does not name a known amount until we know half of what. Most fraction errors in later stages trace back to exactly this: the child compares two fractions and forgets that the whole is different.
>
> **The red line**
> Do not accept "one out of four" as the definition of a quarter without the equality condition. And watch the diagrams in some textbooks: unequal parts labeled as quarters build a wrong concept that is hard to correct.
>
> **How to help?**
> In the kitchen: "This is half a cup. And this is half a pot. Which has more water?" — one question like that helps more than a page of exercises.
>
> **When to review with them?**
> If they answer "equal" for half a cup and half a pot, the concept has not settled. Replay scene six on its own; there is no need to repeat the whole episode.

> 1. Gather three differently-sized containers from the kitchen *(plastic, no glass)*.
> 2. Fill each **to its half with water** — cold tap water only.
> 3. Your child arranges the containers from least water to most, **though all are halves**.
> 4. Ask: "They are all a half, so why do they differ?" and let them explain in their own words.
> 5. Fold a paper strip into halves, then quarters, then eighths, and compare the piece size each time.
>
> **Variation for an older child:** two differently-shaped, equal-valued fractions — 2/6 and 4/12 — prove their equality with paper, not arithmetic.

## Production Notes

- **Equal parts are drawn genuinely equal by measurement**, not by visual approximation. Any deviation ruins the scene 5 lesson.
- The two loaves in scene 2 must have a size difference that is **immediately visible** (a 1:2 ratio at least).
- All numbers are authored teaching examples, and the class and school are imaginary. It is **forbidden** to introduce any real statistic or ratio.
- Displayed numerals follow `numeral_system: auto`, and only the numeric value is stored.
- The second question's answer is not given in scene 7; it is completed in the game.
- No timer · no flashing > 3Hz · no sudden sound · no UI text inside the images.
- ⏳ A pedagogical-mathematics review is required for the sequence of scenes 3 and 4.

## Localization Notes

- Fraction vocabulary (numerator/denominator, quarter, third) translates cleanly; the pedagogy is language-independent.
- English uses regular plurals and natural fraction phrasing ("three quarters", "one third"); the Arabic number-agreement forms were re-phrased naturally rather than translated literally.
- All authored numeric values were preserved exactly.
