## The Question It Answers

> **"A bar taller than another bar… does it mean it is much bigger?"**

## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child reads a bar's value in a bar chart **after examining the scale**, and uses the value to answer a question.

Criterion: reads 5 of 6 values correctly from two charts with different scales, and answers two questions that need two values together (a difference or a sum).

## New Vocabulary

| Word | How it is introduced |
|---|---|
| the axis | the line the numbers or the names sit on |
| the scale | how much each horizontal line represents: one? two? five? |
| the title | what tells you what the chart is about |
| the axis start | which number the axis begins from — not always zero |

## Mastery Criterion

```
Examines the scale before reading, and reads 5 of 6 values correctly from two charts with different scales,
answers two questions that need two values together (a difference and a sum),
and decides that two charts with different axes carry the same values.
Reading the values correctly with only one scale does not count as mastery.
```

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> **Reading a chart from its scale, not from its shape.** A skill they need in science and social studies, and in life afterward. And most important: that **a chart may give a different impression than its numbers** when its axis does not start at zero.
>
> **The red line**
> Do not accept an answer taken from the bar's length by eye. Always ask: "How much does each line represent?" before "What is the value?"
>
> **How to help?**
> At any chart they see — in a textbook or a leaflet — ask three questions: What is the title? What are the axes? Where does the vertical axis start? Three questions are enough.
>
> **A note on the data**
> All the numbers in this episode are **authored for a teaching example**, not a real statistic. If your child asks "Is this really true?", the answer is: no, this is a learning example — and that itself is a good lesson.

---

## Family Activity

> **Two Charts for One Set of Data — 20 minutes**
>
> 1. Count something real at home over five days: the number of cups of water your child drinks, or pages they read.
> 2. Draw a bar chart on squared paper, **with the axis starting at zero**.
> 3. Draw the same chart, **but let the axis start at a number higher** than zero.
> 4. Ask: "Which chart makes the difference look bigger? And did the numbers change?"
> 5. Write a title for each chart, and a unit on the axis. **A chart without a title is crossed out.**
>
> **Variation for an older child:** draw the same data at two different scales (step = 1 and step = 5) and discuss: which scale is clearer and why.
