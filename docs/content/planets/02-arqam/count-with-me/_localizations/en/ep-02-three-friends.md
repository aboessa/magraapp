## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child counts up to 3, and understands that **the last number they said is the count of the whole group**.

> This concept is called the **cardinality principle**. Many children count "one two three" and, when asked "how many?", start counting again — because they did not grasp that "three" is the answer.

Criterion: answers "how many?" with the correct number **without recounting**, in 3 of 4 attempts.

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> Two precise things:
> **First:** that the last number they said is **the count of the whole group**. Many children count correctly, but when asked "how many?" they recount — because they did not link counting to the answer.
> **Second:** that **the number does not change** when things move. A three-year-old often thinks three spread-apart pieces are "more" than three close together.
>
> **A quick test at home:**
> Put 3 pieces close together and ask "how many?" Then **spread them apart in front of them** and ask again "how many now?" If they say "more" or recount, they have not yet mastered conservation. **This is completely normal at this age** and takes time.
>
> **How to help?**
> After each count, ask "how many?" and do not let them recount. If they recount, calmly say: "You said three, so it is three."
>
> **What not to do?**
> Do not rush the counting. **Slow correct counting helps more than fast incomplete counting.** And do not move on to 10 before mastering 3.

## Family Activity

> **Conservation of Number — 10 minutes**
>
> 1. Put 3 grapes in a row in front of your child.
> 2. Ask them to count, then ask: "How many?"
> 3. **Spread them apart in front of them** and ask again: "How many now?"
> 4. If they say "more", do not correct. Recount together slowly.
> 5. Repeat through play over several days — **this is a concept that needs time, not explanation.**
>
> **Most important:** do not turn it into a test. Make it a game at mealtime or tidy-up time.
