## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child compares two sets and decides **more / less / equal**.

The taught method: **one-to-one matching for comparison** — we place an item from the first set beside an item from the second, and whatever remains without a partner shows which is more. This is more accurate and easier than counting for large numbers.

Criterion: compares correctly in 4 of 5 attempts, including one equal case.

### Visual rules

- The frame is **always split** by a thin `#2B3767` divider — it shows there are two sets, not one.
- The matching lines `#00D6F5` are thin and are drawn **one by one**, not all at once.
- The unpaired leftover glows `#FF9F1C` = "look here".
- The larger set glows `#00D6F5` when named.
- In scenes 2, 3, 4, 6: the items are **identical in size**. Scene 5 is **the intentional exception**.

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> Comparison by **matching**, not by counting. We place an item opposite an item, and whatever is left without a partner shows which is more. This is more accurate than counting, and works even with large numbers that are hard to count.
>
> **The common confusion: size and number**
> A four-year-old often thinks two large balls are "more" than three small ones. This is **completely normal** at this age, which is why we added a whole scene to separate the two.
>
> **How to help?**
> When sharing anything — biscuits, grapes — ask them: "Who has more?" Then **match together** piece against piece instead of counting.
>
> **When to worry?**
> If your child after age five insists that big things are always "more in number" even after matching in front of them, re-teach with touch and real distribution. And do not worry if they need time; this concept develops.
>
> **What not to do?**
> Do not use the `>` and `<` symbols now. **Meaning before symbol** by years.

## Family Activity

> **Who Has More? — 10 minutes**
>
> 1. Share grapes or buttons unequally between you.
> 2. Ask: "Who has more?"
> 3. **Do not count.** Put one piece opposite another until one set runs out.
> 4. Whatever is left over shows who has more.
> 5. **The trap game:** give them 3 large pieces and take 5 small ones, and ask. Then match and discover.
>
> **Daily situations:** socks and pegs · spoons and plates · cars and parking spots. Matching is everywhere.

## Localization Notes

- "More in number" is rendered in a way that separates count from size in each language (`more in number`, not `more`). A literal translation loses the pedagogical distinction.
- English uses regular plurals; the Arabic feminine dual constructions ("two equal sets", "two small balls") were re-phrased into natural English.
