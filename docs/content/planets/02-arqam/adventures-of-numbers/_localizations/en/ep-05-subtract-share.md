## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child understands subtraction in both its meanings — **taking away** and **the difference** — and links them to the `−` symbol.

> **Why two meanings?**
> This is a common reason children stumble on subtraction. "I had 7 and ate 3" is taking away. "I have 7 and you have 3, how many more?" is a difference. **The same operation, two completely different contexts.** A child who learned only taking-away is stuck on difference problems.

Criterion: subtracts correctly in 4 of 5 attempts, including one difference problem.

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> That subtraction has **two meanings**, and this is a common reason children stumble:
> **Taking away:** "I had 8 and ate 3, 5 are left." Something actually left.
> **The difference:** "I have 7 and you have 4, I have 3 more." Nothing left, but we subtract.
>
> **A child who learned only taking-away is stuck on difference problems** — they ask "but nothing left!" as Counter did.
>
> **The check:** "Can the remainder be bigger than the start?" A rule that protects your child from the most famous mistake: adding instead of subtracting.
>
> **How to help?**
> Use both contexts together:
> **Taking away:** "You have five grapes, give me two. How many are left?"
> **Difference:** "You have five and I have three. How many more do you have?"
>
> **When to worry?**
> An eight-year-old subtracts up to 10 by counting. Relying on fingers or drawing lines **is normal and healthy**.
>
> **What not to do?**
> Do not say "subtraction is the opposite of addition" and expect them to understand. **Meaning with things first.** And do not make them memorize the subtraction table.

## Family Activity

> **The Biscuit Plate — 15 minutes**
>
> **Part one — taking away:**
> 1. Put 8 pieces on a plate. Let your child count them.
> 2. Take 3 pieces **in front of them**.
> 3. Ask: "How many are left?" then count together.
> 4. Write: 8 − 3 = 5.
>
> **Part two — the difference:**
> 1. Take 4 pieces, and give them 7.
> 2. Ask: "How many more do you have?"
> 3. **Match** piece against piece, and count the remainder.
> 4. Write: 7 − 4 = 3.
>
> **Ask them at the end:** "The second time, did anything leave?" — let them discover that subtraction works in both cases.
>
> **The check game:** say "6 − 2 = 8" and let them find that it does not make sense and explain why.
