## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child compares two sets and determines the **numeric difference** between them, not only which is more.

The difference from the sprouts episode: there it was "which is more?". Here it is **"how many more?"** — a shift from qualitative to quantitative comparison, and a direct lead-in to subtraction.

Criterion: identifies the more and **the difference** in 3 of 4 comparisons.

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> Three things:
> **The numeric difference:** not "which is more" but "how many more". This is a direct lead-in to subtraction.
> **The reasonableness check:** when Counter said the difference was "ten" between 6 and 4, Noma asked: "Can the difference be bigger than the set?" This skill saves your child from accepting any result without thinking.
> **Multiple solutions:** to make two sets equal, either add to the smaller or move from the larger. Mathematics has ways, not one way.
>
> **A subtle point:** moving one item from the more to the less reduces the difference **by two**, not by one. Do not explain this verbally — let them try it by hand many times and discover it.
>
> **How to help?**
> When sharing anything: "You have five, and I have three. How many more do you have?" then **match**, do not count.
>
> **When to worry?**
> A seven-year-old should find the difference between two small sets by matching. If they compute randomly with no check, train them to ask: "Does this make sense?"
>
> **What not to do?**
> Do not teach them "the operation" (7 − 4 = 3) before they have seen the difference with their own eyes many times. **Meaning before symbol.**

## Family Activity

> **Who Has More, and By How Many? — 15 minutes**
>
> 1. Share small things between you unequally.
> 2. **Match** a thing opposite a thing until the smaller set runs out.
> 3. Count what is left — that is the difference.
> 4. Now the fun part: **"How do we make them equal?"** Try both ways: adding and moving.
> 5. **An advanced question:** "Why did moving one reduce the difference by two?" Let them try and discover.
>
> **The check game:** say a clearly wrong answer ("The difference is twenty!") and let them find that it does not make sense.
