## The Question

> **“Why is the weather here different from the weather there?”**

## Learning Objective

**One goal:** explains a weather difference between two places using **the angle of incoming sunlight and proximity to water**.

Criterion: when given two unfamiliar places, identifies which of the two causes explains the difference and says why, in 3 of 4 attempts without help.

## New Vocabulary

| Word | Presented as |
|---|---|
| angle of incoming sunlight | “Is the sunlight nearly overhead or arriving at a slant?” |
| energy concentration | “The same light spread over a smaller or larger area” |
| moderation | “Nearby water slows temperature change” |
| temperature range | “The difference between the day’s highest and lowest temperatures” |

| Stable principles | A more oblique sunlight angle spreads energy across a larger area; nearby water changes temperature more slowly than dry land |

> **Editorial rule:** this edition teaches relationships, not memorized values. “Wider,” “narrower,” “twice,” and “half” remain relative. Every place name is fictional, every illustrative comparison is labeled, and no real place inherits a made-up climate claim.

## Mastery Criterion

```text
Given two unfamiliar places, identifies which of the two taught causes explains the difference and explains how,
in 3 of 4 attempts without help. The learner must distinguish the causes: sunlight angle helps explain the
level of warming, while nearby water helps explain the range of change. When neither is sufficient, the learner
states that another cause is needed rather than forcing the two-cause model.
```

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> Weather differences have causes that can be named and tested. This episode gives two useful causes and, just as importantly, teaches that they do not explain every case.
>
> **The two causes answer different questions**
> - **Angle of incoming sunlight:** How concentrated is the same incoming energy? A slanting beam spreads over more ground.
> - **Nearby water:** How much does temperature change between day and night? Water warms and cools more slowly, narrowing a coastal place’s daily range.
>
> This distinction is the center of the episode. Saying only “both affect weather” is not yet mastery.
>
> **A common misconception**
> A child may say a place is cooler because it is “farther from the Sun.” That is not the explanation here. The model keeps the Sun distant and the rays parallel: the difference is the **angle** at which the rays meet a curved Earth.
>
> **Why there are no numbered temperatures**
> Every place is invented and every comparison is relative. The child learns the relationship instead of memorizing a number with no real source.
>
> **Why the limits section matters**
> Elevation, winds, ocean currents, rain shadows, and vegetation also matter. A useful model can be incomplete without being wrong. Recognizing when a third cause is needed is part of geographic reasoning.
>
> **Not covered here**
> Climate change over time is outside this episode’s scope. This lesson compares places; it does not explain change across years.
>
> **Ask afterward**
> “Which would usually have a wider day–night range: a coastal town or a dry inland town? Why?” Listen for the reason, not only the choice.

## Family Activity

> **Water and dry material — about 40 minutes, mostly waiting**
>
> 1. Use two matching plastic containers. Put room-temperature water in one and dry sand or soil in the other.
> 2. Place them side by side in sunlight. If sunlight is unavailable, an adult may position a desk lamp at least a hand-span away.
> 3. Compare warmth by holding the back of a hand just above each surface, without putting a hand into either container. A household thermometer may be used if available.
> 4. Record a relative observation every ten minutes: which changes faster?
> 5. Move both containers to shade and continue observing every ten minutes. Which cools faster?
> 6. Draw two simple curves with no temperature units: one for water and one for the dry material.
>
> **Expected observation:** dry material generally warms faster and cools faster; water changes more slowly. The cooling phase is essential because the lesson concerns the **range**, not only daytime warming.
>
> **Safety:** An adult handles, switches, and positions the lamp. Keep all electrical equipment away from water. Do not touch the bulb or shade. Use plastic, not glass; use room-temperature water; never leave a lamp operating unattended.
>
> **Extension for age 12:** Add a third dry container covered with white paper. Compare its curve and discuss which omitted factor it resembles: surface cover and reflection.
