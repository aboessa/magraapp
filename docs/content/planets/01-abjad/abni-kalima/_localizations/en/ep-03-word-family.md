## The Question It Answers

> **"Why do some words sound alike at the end?"**

## Learning Objective

**One objective:** the child groups words that belong to the same **rime family** — words that end with the same sound-and-spelling chunk (like *-at*) — leaning on the shared ending they can *hear* first, then the shared letters they can *see*.

Criterion: sorts 5 of 6 words into two correct families, rejects one lookalike that does not belong, and says why it was rejected.

**Note on re-authoring:** English does not have the Arabic triliteral root. The native English idea here is the **rime** (the vowel + what follows it in a syllable) shared across a *word family*: `-at` → cat, hat, mat. We teach families the child can hear, not roots.

## New Vocabulary

| Word | How it is introduced |
|---|---|
| word family | words that end with the same sound and the same letters |
| rhyming end | the part of the word we hear the same, like *-at* in cat and hat |
| a lookalike | a word that shares one letter but not the ending — it does not belong |

## Parent Guide

> **What is your child learning?**
> **Word families** — that English builds many words from one rhyming end: *-at* → cat, hat, mat, bat. A child who reads "cat" and hears the family can read "mat" and "bat" faster, because they are no longer starting from zero with every new word.
>
> **The red line**
> Do not let them sort by shape. Two words that share one letter are not a family. The test: **the ending sounds the same, and the ending letters are the same.**
>
> **How to help?**
> With any new word in the street or a book, ask: "Can you say a word in its family?" — "hop… top… mop." This is the strongest reading habit they can build.
>
> **When to worry?**
> A sense of word families starts incomplete and grows to about age ten. Do not expect it finished now, and do not explain "vowel" or spelling rules — the time has not come.

## Family Activity

> **The Rhyme Tree — 10 minutes**
>
> 1. Write one rhyming end in the middle of a sheet: **-op**.
> 2. Take turns writing every word you know in its family: hop · top · mop · stop.
> 3. For each word, your child says **what it means** before writing it.
> 4. **The fun trap:** suggest a lookalike that does not belong, like "cop out of family" words such as "cape", and let them catch it.
> 5. No winner and no timer.
>
> **Variation for an older child:** try two more endings — *-at* then *-ig* — and compare: which family is bigger?
