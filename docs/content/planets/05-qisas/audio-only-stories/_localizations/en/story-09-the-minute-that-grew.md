## Characters and Performance

- **Narrator:** close to the heroine's thinking, with no preaching.
- **Dania:** 10 years old, hardworking and nervous before the presentation.
- **Reem:** a practical, calm classmate.

🔴 **Name adaptation:** "دانيا" → **Dania**, "ريم" → **Reem**, substituted locally where needed.

## Full Recording Script

**[A faint wall clock, papers arranged quickly]**

One minute was left before Dania's presentation to the class. Only one minute, but it felt as if it grew every time she looked at the clock.

She arranged her cards: the first about the idea, the second about the experiment, the third about the result. Then she mixed them up by accident. She put them back in order, but she was no longer sure which card had been first.

She said to herself: "I'll forget the beginning. And if I forget the beginning, I'll forget everything. And if I forget everything…"

Reem, sitting near her, said: "Stop at the first 'if.'"

Dania looked at her: "What?"

Reem said: "You are in the real minute now, not in all the things that might happen after it. What do you need in this minute?"

Dania answered quickly: "Arrange the cards, check the numbers, drink water, and change the last sentence."

Reem said: "That's more than a minute. Choose one thing that is stopping you from starting."

Dania turned over the first card and found on its back many notes she had written last night: two definitions, two examples, and three alternative sentences. She said: "All of this is important."

Reem said: "It may be important for the project, but it is not all important for the start of the presentation. The beginning needs one sentence that tells us what you tested."

Dania put the extra cards in the folder instead of holding them in a shaking hand.

Dania looked at the cards. She said: "I forgot their order."

Reem wrote one word at the top of each card in pencil: idea, experiment, result.

She said: "The first obstacle is over."

Dania asked: "And the numbers?"

"They're on the second card."

"And the water?"

Reem handed her the bottle. Dania took one sip.

"And the last sentence?"

Reem said: "Not necessary now. Start with what you have."

The teacher called Dania's name.

She stood. The minute suddenly returned to its natural size; sixty seconds, no more, and they had already passed.

She put the cards on the table and began: "Today I'll show a small experiment to learn which kind of paper holds more weight when we fold it."

She heard her voice clearly. She moved to the second card. She read a number wrong, then stopped and said: "Sorry, the correct number is six."

Nothing scary happened. One student wrote a note, and the teacher nodded for her to continue.

At the end she lifted the paper model, and explained that the shape of the fold helped it hold more weight. A question she had not expected came: "Did you try more than one fold?"

She said: "No, I tried only one shape. That could be our next test."

Then another student asked: "Do you expect the same result with thicker paper?" Dania looked at her teacher, then at her model, and said: "I don't know yet. Paper thickness is a new variable, and if we change it we need a new experiment." The answer was not saved on any card, and yet it was the clearest sentence she said.

When she sat down, she said to Reem: "I didn't say the nice last sentence."

Reem said: "But your last answer was better; it was honest and it opened a new experiment."

After class, Dania thought about the minute that had seemed huge. The problem was not that time was slow, but that her mind had put inside it the whole presentation, and all the possible mistakes, and even questions that were not asked.

She wrote three small columns on the last card: "Now," "After the presentation," and "Not necessary." She put arranging the cards in the Now column, reviewing a new design in the After column, and the ornate sentence in the Not-necessary column. The columns did not solve all her tension, but they gave each thought its right time.

The next week, Reem had to read a paragraph on the school radio. Before her turn she said: "I feel the minute won't end."

Dania smiled and asked her: "What is the one thing stopping you from starting?"

Reem said: "I don't know where to take a breath."

Dania put two small marks between the two long sentences and said: "Try here and here."

Reem breathed and began.

And from that day, not every minute became easy. Some stayed heavy, and some passed quickly. But Dania knew a question that returns the minute to its size: what is the one thing I need now in order to start?

**[The ticking fades, a calm breath, an ending]**

## Mixing Direction

- The clock's tick does not speed up and no heartbeat is amplified; the tension comes from the thinking and the speaking.
- Reading the number wrong is delivered as an ordinary stumble, not an embarrassing situation.
- The practical sentence is the story's core, but it is not repeated like an advertising slogan.

## Parent Guide

> **A ~370-second audio story for a 9–12 child**
>
> An audio-only story — no pictures. A nervous minute before a presentation grows huge in Dania's mind; a friend brings it back to size with one question: "What is the one thing you need now, in order to start?"
>
> **What it gives your child:** a way to handle pre-performance anxiety — separating "now" from "everything that might happen after" — with no lecture. The honest, unscripted answer turns out to be the best one.
>
> **How to use it?** A daytime listen, useful before a test or a talk. The question is worth keeping: "What is the one thing you need now, to start?"
