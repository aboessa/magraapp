## Characters and Performance

- **Narrator:** cinematic in moderation, describing sounds and distances precisely.
- **Sara:** 11 years old, practical and observant of detail.
- **Mr. Nader:** a calm librarian who respects her reasoning and does not give the answer at once.

🔴 **Name adaptation:** "سارة" → **Sara**, "الأستاذ نادر" → **Mr. Nader**, substituted locally where needed.

## Full Recording Script

**[Rain behind glass, a page turning, a distant clock]**

Sara stayed in the neighborhood library until ten minutes before closing. The rain was heavy, so her father sent word that he was waiting for her at the entrance. She closed her book and put it back on the cart, then noticed that the lamp above the last table was still on.

Mr. Nader said from behind the loans desk: "I turned off all the hall lamps. Why is this one still on?"

He pressed the switch. The lamp went off for a moment, then came back.

Sara said: "Maybe the switch is faulty."

He answered: "Maybe. We won't take it apart or touch the wires. But we can observe what happens."

They stood at a safe distance. He pressed the switch again. The lamp went off, and after two seconds a faint sound was heard under the table.

**[A small electronic click, then the light returning with a soft power-on sound]**

Sara said: "There is something restarting it."

She bent a little without reaching out her hand. She saw a white box connected to the lamp, and on its side a small hole.

Mr. Nader passed in front of the table, and the lamp went off. He moved away, and it came back after two seconds.

Sara said: "It's not an ordinary switch. Maybe a motion sensor… but it does the opposite."

Mr. Nader searched the device log on the computer. He found an old line: "Last-table lamp — experimental timer to remind the reader to return books."

He said: "A volunteer installed it years ago. If the table stays still, it lights. And if a person moves in front of it, it dims a little."

Sara asked: "And why would a reader need a lamp that bothers them?"

They opened the nearby drawer, and found a small card: "If the lamp lights, look around. Did you leave a book outside the return place?"

Sara looked at the table. There was no book on it. But under the chair she heard the rub of paper when the air moved the edge of a page.

**[A light page flutter]**

There was a thin book that had fallen behind the bag. Mr. Nader picked it up and put it on the return cart.

Sara said: "The lamp solved its own riddle."

Mr. Nader took out the closing checklist, and read it aloud: the windows, the computers, the book cart, the lost-and-found box. Sara said: "The list does not mention the last table." So he added with his pen: "Check the last table without touching the device." The note was not a solution to the riddle, but it kept the person closing the library the next day from forgetting it.

Sara said: "Sometimes we don't need a stronger memory, but a place to put what we don't want to forget."

But the lamp came back to light once more.

Mr. Nader said: "Then the riddle is not over."

They searched around the table without moving the devices. Sara found a pen between two shelves, an old loan card, and a sheet with half a sentence: "To whoever finds this…"

She turned the sheet over. On the back was written: "Do not leave anything behind you, except a useful idea."

Mr. Nader laughed: "It seems the volunteer liked mysterious messages."

Sara put the pen in the lost-and-found box and the sheet in the library file. Then Mr. Nader pressed the main stop button on the box as the device manual explained. The lamp went off, and did not come back.

Mr. Nader called Sara's father at the entrance to tell him they would need two more minutes to finish closing. Then he placed a small barrier in front of the table so no one would use the device before it was checked. They did not try to fix something they did not understand; they did only what they could do safely: observe, record, and prevent use.

He said: "We'll ask the technician to check it tomorrow. The old device may not be suitable now."

Sara asked: "But will we remove it?"

He thought a moment and said: "We remove the old wires, and keep the idea."

The next week the library placed a small sign near the exit: "Before you leave: a book to the return cart, a lost thing to the box, and a useful idea with you."

There was no longer a need for a lamp that fought its switch. Readers began to pause a moment on their own.

As for Sara, whenever she closed a book she asked herself: what is the thing I will put back in its place, and what is the idea that will leave with me?

**[A book closing quietly, the rain stopping, an ending]**

## Mixing Direction

- The mystery is based on observation, with no electrical danger; the characters do not touch the wires and the device is referred to a technician.
- The power-on sound is faint, with no flicker or annoying repetition in the front.
- The written text is not read in a mysterious voice; the narrator presents it naturally.

## Parent Guide

> **A ~360-second audio story for a 9–12 child**
>
> An audio-only story — no pictures. A lamp that won't turn off becomes a small mystery, solved by careful observation — and, importantly, by **not** touching the wires and referring the device to a technician.
>
> **What it gives your child:** an investigative habit that is also a safety habit — observe, record, prevent use, and hand an unknown device to an expert. There is no lesson lectured; the closing checklist and the "useful idea" sign carry it.
>
> **How to use it?** A daytime listen. The safety point ("don't take apart or touch wiring you don't understand — tell an adult") is worth saying plainly once, apart from the story.
