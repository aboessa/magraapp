## The Arc

Sami runs in the neighborhood, and Mazin draws on the step. Each tries to pull the other into his own game and fails. Then they understand that a game can bring them together: Mazin draws a **track**, and Sami runs on it. So they sit together in the evening.

## 🔴 Two decisions that distinguish this story

### 1. **No one changes to suit the other**

| ❌ Rejected form | ✅ Form used |
|---|---|
| Mazin learns to run | 🔴 **Mazin stays a drawer** |
| Sami learns to sit | 🔴 **Sami stays a runner** |
| One gives in | 🔴 **They find one thing that brings both together** |

> 🔴 **This is the most common mistake in "accepting difference" stories:** they end with the quiet child having become loud, so the message reads: **be like the majority.**
>
> In this story **each stays as they are**, and the solution comes from **combining the two differences**, not erasing one. Mazin draws the track, and Sami runs on it — **each does what they love, in the same game.**

### 2. 🔴 The difference is in the **preference**, not the identity

| What we chose | What we rejected |
|---|---|
| ✅ loves movement / loves calm | ❌ a different language |
| ✅ a personal preference | ❌ a disability as a lesson subject |
| ✅ varies between children | ❌ shape, color, or country |

> 🔴 **We refused to build the story on an identity.** A child with another language or a disability **appears in this series as an ordinary character in ordinary situations** — not as a lesson subject in accepting difference.
>
> **Making them the lesson subject produces a stereotype** even with good intent: they become "the other" who is accepted, not a person with their own preferences like everyone.
>
> And a personal preference *(movement or calm)* **is a real difference every child lives**, it can change, and it does not classify anyone.

## Full Narration Script for Recording

```
P1.  This is Sami. He lives at the end of the street.
     He runs from morning to evening.
P2.  And this is Mazin. His new neighbor.
     Calm and cheerful, and he loves to draw.
P3.  Sami said: let's run together.
     Mazin said: I prefer to sit.
P4.  Mazin said: let's draw together.
     Sami said: I don't like sitting for long.
P5.  So Mazin sat drawing alone.
     And Sami ran alone.
P6.  Sami tried to draw a little. He got tired.
     And Mazin tried to run. He got tired too.
P7.  Mazin looked at his big sheet.
     And said: I draw, and you run.
P8.  So Mazin drew lines on the ground.
     Long lines that curve and return.
P9.  The street had a running track.
     Sami ran on it, and Mazin drew more.
P10. Sami did not change, and Mazin did not change.
     But they played together.
P11. They sat on the step as the evening calmed.
     And the track is still on the ground.
P12. And now each one sleeps in his home.
```

**12 pages · 27 sentences · each sentence ≤ 12 words.**

## Parent Guide

> **A ~4:10-minute story about playing together without being alike**
>
> Sami loves running, and Mazin loves drawing. Each invites the other to his own game and is refused. Then they understand: **Mazin draws a track, and Sami runs on it.**
>
> **🔴 No one changes**
> The tenth sentence: "**Sami did not change, and Mazin did not change. But they played together.**"
>
> And this is deliberate. The most common mistake in "accepting difference" stories is to end with **the quiet child having become loud** — so the message reads: be like the majority.
>
> Here **each stays as they are**, and the solution comes from **combining the two differences**, not erasing one.
>
> **🔴 And the difference is in preference, not identity**
> We chose a difference every child lives: **movement or calm**. And we refused to build the story on a language, a shape, or a disability.
>
> **The reason:** making an identity the lesson subject produces a stereotype even with good intent — the child becomes "the other" who is accepted, not a person with their own preferences like everyone. And in this series, children with disabilities appear **as ordinary characters in ordinary situations**, not as lessons.
>
> **The image carries the message**
> On page five **the frame is split**: Mazin on the right and Sami on the left. And on ten **one frame brings them together**. Your child will feel the difference without noticing it.
>
> **And Mazin is cheerful, not sad**
> The quiet child in this story **is not alone and not pitiable**. Pity spoils the message, because it makes calm a deficiency.
>
> **Two new words:** **cheerful** and **track**. In context, and the image explains the track. **No explanation and no question.**
>
> **🔴 No questions and no game after the story** and no "next story."
>
> **If your child is quiet and you worry about them?**
> Calm is **a preference, not a problem**. And this story tells them their preference is accepted, and that friendship does not need them to change. **And if you see real, ongoing withdrawal — not merely calm — consult a specialist.**
>
> **The link to the values planet**
> A sibling of the episode [A Kind Word](../../../04-qiyam/hekaya-wa-hikma/ep-05-kind-word.md) — both say: **the invitation is short, and friendship does not need sameness.**

## Family Activity — daytime

> 🔴 **For the daytime, not after the story.**
>
> **A game both of them love — 20 minutes**
>
> 1. Each one writes on a sheet **something they love** in play: running · drawing · building · talking · music.
> 2. Read the sheets together. **The differences will be clear.**
> 3. The question: "**How do we play a game with what each of us loves in it?**"
> 4. Invent one game that combines at least two of the written things.
> 5. Actually play it.
>
> **The third step is the activity.** The child who invents a game combining their preference with someone else's learns what advice cannot teach.
>
> 🔴 **Without forcing anyone to leave what they love.** If the game does not work, the attempt is enough.
>
> **A short talk:** "Do you have a friend who loves things other than what you love?" then: "**And what do you do together?**" — the second question is the important one.
